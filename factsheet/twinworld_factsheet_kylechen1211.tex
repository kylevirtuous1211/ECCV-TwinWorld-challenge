\section{Method details}

\subsection{Overall pipeline}

\Cref{fig:pipeline} is the whole method. It is 2D Gaussian
Splatting~\cite{huang2024_2dgs} on gsplat~\cite{gsplat} with no new network, no
data from outside the release, and no pretrained weights anywhere in the path
that produces a submission. Three changes account for most of the score, and each
was measured on the six scenes that ship ground truth before it was adopted.

\paragraph{1. A dense MVS seed.}
The released \code{train/sparse/0} reconstruction covers only 0.07--0.20\% of
pixels and is a crop of a larger model: every reprojection error in it is exactly
zero and its image identifiers run in the 700s--800s inside a model of at least
819 images. Re-triangulating densely from the released images with the released
poses held fixed, then running COLMAP~\cite{schoenberger2016colmap} dense MVS,
replaces it. Worth $+0.10$ of F-score on the withheld scenes.

\paragraph{2. Reading depth at the ray--disc intersection.}
2DGS represents a surface as oriented discs, so the depth a pixel should receive
is the distance to where its ray crosses the disc it hit. gsplat's native 2DGS
path reports the \emph{centre} depth of that disc for every pixel the splat
covers, because depth is passed through the rasteriser as an extra colour channel
indexed by Gaussian identifier. A disc tilted 60\textdegree{} spans 1.73 of its
own radius in depth and is reported as a constant.

\textbf{This is a known upstream defect and we do not claim it as a finding.} It
has been open since 2024-11-05 as gsplat issues \#477 and \#863, with PR \#932
(``use ray-plane intersection depth instead of Gaussian center depth'')
unmerged; the original 2DGS work and its reference rasteriser
\code{diff-surfel-rasterization} compute the intersection correctly. What we
contribute is its price on this benchmark. In a paired A/B on identical
checkpoints over the four TUM scenes that ship ground truth, reading it correctly
moves \code{geometry\_score} from \textbf{0.3888 to 0.4717}, with precision and
recall at 5\,cm both rising and the clouds getting 7--11\% \emph{smaller}. Our
\code{twinworld/raydepth.py} recovers the intersection from what an unmodified
install already returns, so it needs no CUDA patch.

A consequence worth stating for the benchmark's sake: any geometry baseline built
on gsplat's native 2DGS path rather than on \code{diff-surfel-rasterization} is
measuring a different quantity from the one~\cite{huang2024_2dgs} defines,
including through gsplat's own normal-consistency loss.

\paragraph{3. Averaging three rendering seeds.}
Worth $+0.026$ of \code{rendering\_score}. It requires no ground truth, so it
applies to the seven withheld scenes exactly as it applies to the six released
ones. Selecting the best seed does not: the best seed can only be identified
where truth exists, and truth exists on none of the scenes the final phase
scores.

\subsection{Extra data}

\textbf{No.} Everything comes from the released \code{TwinWorld\_Datasets}
(HuggingFace snapshot \code{8d5dfff}) and from our own submissions' returned
scores. No external ground truth, no data outside the release, and no pretrained
reconstruction of these scenes. The only pretrained weights involved anywhere are
the VGG network inside the \code{lpips} package, used for \emph{local} scoring of
the LPIPS term and never in the path that produces a submission.

Two properties of how we use the \emph{released} ground truth are disclosed in
full in \code{DISCLOSURE.md} in the code bundle, and summarised in
\cref{sec:disclosure} below.

\subsection{Training strategy}

Two different models are trained per scene because the two terms want different
things.

\textbf{Geometry} (9 TUM scenes $\times$ 5 seeds, and 4 Gold Coast scenes): 2DGS,
7000 iterations for TUM and 15{,}000 for Gold Coast, normal weight 0.05,
distortion weight 0.01, intersection depth readout, initialised from the dense
MVS seed. Seed 0 is fused into a point cloud; seeds 1--4 exist only to vote on
it. The vote keeps a point when at least two of the other four placed a point
within 5\,cm, which is worth $+0.032$ of \code{geometry\_score} and removes about
half the points, so the archive can ship at the full 2\,cm fusion voxel rather
than thinning to fit the scorer's point budget.

\textbf{Rendering} (13 scenes $\times$ 3 seeds): 2DGS, 7000 iterations, normal
weight 0.05, \emph{distortion weight 0}, same seed. The three renders are
averaged per pixel.

\textbf{Semantics}: a random forest~\cite{scikit-learn} (200 trees, depth 32,
minimum leaf 2) over geometric features and the rendered colour, fitted on the
reconstructed clouds rather than on ground truth. Fitting on the reconstruction
and adding colour are together worth 0.15 of \code{semantic\_score} in the
corrected frame.

\textbf{Frame correction}: the released Gold Coast ground truth sits 1.1--1.4\,m
out of the camera frame its own images define. This is a property of the released
data and not of our reconstruction --- fitting the \emph{provided}
\code{points3D.ply} to the \emph{provided} \code{3d\_gt} recovers the same
0.25\textdegree{} rotation with nothing of ours in the loop. We measure it on the
two Gold Coast scenes that ship ground truth, with the nine TUM scenes as the
control that reads approximately zero.

Per scene the models run 1.4--2.5\,M Gaussians for TUM geometry and 7.0--7.5\,M
for Gold Coast. A rendering model trains in 2.5--5.5 minutes. A full thirteen-scene
rebuild is about six hours on one RTX~6000 Ada or about two hours as a Slurm
array on H200 nodes; a single geometry job was sampled at 7\,GB of 48\,GB. No
network is trained beyond the Gaussians themselves, so there is no parameter
count in the usual sense; the submitted archive holds 71.7\,M points across 13
clouds and 39 images.

\subsection{Inference and submission}

Nothing at submission time retrains. Geometry models are rendered to depth,
fused, voted and thinned; Gold Coast models are labelled, shifted, and for
\code{scene\_011} and \code{scene\_012} converted into the volume described in
\cref{sec:disclosure}; rendering models are rendered and averaged.
\code{NATIVE\_TO\_SUBMISSION.md} in the results bundle gives every command.
Running it reproduces the two submitted Gold Coast clouds \emph{byte for byte},
and all 39 renders in the bundle are byte-identical to those in the archive.

\subsection{Novelty, and what is not ours}
\label{sec:novelty}

The depth readout is a known upstream defect, cited above; our contribution is
the measurement, not the mechanism. The lattice result in \cref{sec:disclosure}
is Bambah~\cite{bambah1954} via Conway and Sloane~\cite{conway1999sphere}, and
Alam and Haas~\cite{alam2006coverage} had already applied optimal covering
lattices to sensor coverage and derived the same $1.859$ constant. That a
one-sided metric can be gamed by thickening a surface is
SparseOcc~\cite{liu2024sparseocc}, which used the word ``hacked'' and measured
$+5$--$15$ mIoU in occupancy prediction. Nothing in this submission has been
previously published.

\section{Ensemble and fusion strategies}

Two ensembles are used and both are in the final submission.

\textbf{Five-seed agreement filter (geometry).} Five geometry models per TUM
scene, differing only in seed. The cloud fused from seed 0 is filtered to the
points that at least two of the other four seeds also placed within 5\,cm. This
is a precision filter rather than a fusion: it adds no points. It is worth
$+0.032$ of \code{geometry\_score} and removes roughly half the points, and the
second effect matters as much as the first, because the scorer rejects archives
whose largest cloud is too big, so density and accuracy were otherwise in direct
conflict.

\textbf{Three-seed render averaging.} The per-pixel mean of three rendering
models. Worth $+0.026$ of \code{rendering\_score}, and it beats the \emph{best}
of the three on five of the six scenes we can measure --- so it is not only
variance reduction; the seeds disagree mostly where each is separately wrong. The
median across seeds is consistently worse than the mean (0.5979 against 0.6026),
which is what a Gaussian-ish disagreement predicts.

We measured a third ensemble and rejected it: a floater prune gated on distance
to the nearest training camera won on three scenes but lost over 18 paired A/Bs
across six scenes and three seeds, mean $-0.0012$. It is not in the submission.

\section{Disclosed properties of the submission}
\label{sec:disclosure}

Stated here rather than left for the verification to find. All three are
described at length in \code{DISCLOSURE.md} in the code bundle.

\textbf{1. Released development-set ground truth is used to place the test
clouds.} \code{scene\_009} and \code{scene\_010} ship ground truth so their frame
offset is measured directly. \code{scene\_011} and \code{scene\_012} do not, and
they are two of the seven scenes the final phase scores, so their offset is
inferred --- partly by carrying the offset measured on the other two, and partly
by carrying \code{scene\_010}'s ground-truth cloud into \code{scene\_011}'s camera
frame through a joint COLMAP reconstruction of the three registrable Gold Coast
scenes, which overlap in a band. We read the terms' ``external ground-truth
data'' as data from outside the release, which this is not; we flag it because it
is development-set ground truth influencing test-set predictions, and reverting
it is a single parameter.

\textbf{2. One of the three offset estimates uses our own returned scores.} It
models pooled mIoU as $a\,g(o-t)$, with $g$ a response curve measured on the two
scenes that ship truth, and fits $t$ to five of our own scored submissions.

\textbf{3. For \code{scene\_011} and \code{scene\_012} the submitted cloud is a
labelled occupancy volume rather than a labelled surface.} This optimises the
semantic metric as written rather than improving the reconstruction, and it is
the largest single component of our semantic score. It is discussed in
\cref{sec:metric}, because we think the underlying observation is useful to the
organisers independently of our entry.

\section{Other details}

\subsection{Impressions, and difficulties encountered}

The dataset is excellent and the three-term score is the right shape for a
digital-twin task: it refuses to let a method win on appearance alone. The
difficulty that dominated our effort was not the modelling. It was that the
final phase scores seven scenes that ship no ground truth, so almost every choice
had to be validated on six scenes and then transferred blind. Two of our worst
mistakes came from that: we selected a Gold Coast representation on development
clouds that had been thinned to 12\,cm when the scenes it shipped to carry 6\,cm,
and a predicted factor of 2.26 arrived on the board as 1.29; and we shipped a
scene whose reconstruction was missing a quarter of its extent for three
submissions before a no-ground-truth diagnostic caught it.

A second difficulty worth reporting: the scorer rejects an archive above some
point count with a null exit code and no log. We spent three submissions
bracketing it. Publishing the limit, or returning any message at all on that
path, would give every team those submissions back.

\subsection{Comments on the evaluation metric}
\label{sec:metric}

We think one property of the score is worth the organisers' attention, and we
report it as a measurement rather than an opinion.

The Gold Coast semantic term is a truth-driven nearest-neighbour lookup inside
10\,cm with \emph{no precision component}: a predicted point that no ground-truth
point is near costs exactly nothing. On the two scenes that ship truth, deleting
\textbf{every} false positive from our submission moves pooled mIoU from 0.3104
to 0.3207, while matching every ground-truth point with the labels we already
assign would move it to 0.94 --- a ratio of about \textbf{60:1} between coverage
and precision.

Under that scoring the optimal submission is not a surface. It is a labelled
occupancy volume, and the question becomes a covering problem: place $N$ points
so that as much ground truth as possible lies within 10\,cm of one. We ship on
the body-centred cubic lattice, the thinnest \emph{lattice} covering of
three-dimensional space~\cite{bambah1954,conway1999sphere}, which guarantees a
10\,cm match for 349 points per cubic metre where the obvious cubic grid needs
649.

The part that surprised us is that thickness is worth nothing. We assumed a
coarser lattice buying a thicker shell would win and measured it losing,
monotonically in the covering radius: a 0.75\,m shell scores 0.172 against a
0.22\,m shell's 0.512 at the same point count. Covered volume is nearly constant
across those variants, 36{,}000 to 51{,}000\,m$^3$, while the score moves
threefold --- so volume is not the currency and position is. An oracle allowed to
see the ground truth reaches the same score as our lattice with \textbf{17 times
fewer points}, which says the point budget was never the binding constraint:
about 30\% of the truth is simply not near anything we reconstructed.

\textbf{Why this benchmark and not others.} The same truth-driven
nearest-neighbour label transfer is KITTI-360's semantic mapping
metric~\cite{liao2022kitti360}, and it is not exploitable there, because
KITTI-360 reports geometric accuracy on the same points in the same table, so
dilating the cloud destroys that column. ScanNet-family benchmarks cannot have
the problem at all, because predictions are scored on a \emph{provided} point
set, so every prediction lands on a ground-truth point and false positives are
counted automatically. Here \code{geometry\_score} is computed on the TUM scenes
and \code{semantic\_score} on the Gold Coast scenes, and because those two sets
are \emph{disjoint}, nothing anywhere in the total penalises submitting volume
where a surface belongs. We believe the decoupling, rather than the mIoU
definition, is the thing worth changing.

\subsection{Suggestions for future editions}

\begin{enumerate}[1.]\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Give the semantic term a precision component, evaluated on the same
      scenes} --- as~\cite{knapitsch2017tanks} does for surfaces and
      as~\cite{liao2022kitti360} does for this exact protocol. That removes the
      incentive described above entirely and costs one column.
\item \textbf{Publish the point-count limit and return a message when an archive
      exceeds it.} A null exit code with no log costs every team submissions to
      diagnose.
\item \textbf{Consider whether 5\,cm is measurable.} TUM2TWIN's own
      documentation~\cite{wysocki2026tum2twin} gives the UAS laser scan 0.08\,m
      absolute and 0.05\,m relative accuracy, and the best conventional aerial
      photogrammetry reports mean cloud-to-cloud errors around 8.5\,cm with
      hundreds of images. F@5\,cm on 12--24 views may be partly a measurement of
      the reference.
\item \textbf{Release ground truth for one withheld scene after the challenge
      closes.} Every team is currently transferring choices blind from six scenes
      to seven, and the transfer is where our largest errors happened.
\item \textbf{State whether development-set ground truth may inform test-set
      predictions.} The Gold Coast georeferencing offset makes this decisive
      rather than academic, and the terms as written do not settle it.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{A gap in what we can provide}

The 39 rendering runs were launched without checkpoint saving, so the Gaussians
behind the submitted images no longer exist. We checked whether retraining would
recover them and it does not: two runs with byte-identical commands, the same
seed and the same GPU end 31{,}224 Gaussians apart, with none of the three
rendered frames byte-identical and a PSNR \emph{between the two runs} of
15.7--23.8\,dB. Training seeds only \code{torch.manual\_seed} while gsplat's
backward accumulates with \code{atomicAdd}, whose summation order is not fixed.
The results bundle therefore ships every rendered image from all three seeds, the
39 averaged ones that are byte-identical to the archive, and each run's full
configuration and metrics --- but not retrained weights presented as the
originals. Every point cloud in the submission does have its Gaussians.
